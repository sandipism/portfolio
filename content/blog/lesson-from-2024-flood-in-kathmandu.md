## Lessons from 2024 flood

In late September 2024, Kathmandu Valley experienced one of the most devastating flood events in its recent history. Days of intense rainfall triggered widespread flooding, landslides, infrastructure damage, and loss of life across the valley and surrounding catchments. Hundreds of families were displaced, major transport routes were disrupted, and entire communities faced the consequences of a disaster that many had considered unlikely in an urban center like Kathmandu.

While extreme rainfall was the immediate trigger, the flood revealed a deeper and more uncomfortable reality: disasters are rarely caused by nature alone. The September 2024 flood exposed long-standing vulnerabilities in urban planning, watershed management, infrastructure development, and disaster preparedness.

![Flooding across the Kathmandu Valley in September 2024](/images/blog/kathmandu-flood-2.jpg)

### Lesson 1: Extreme Rainfall Is No Longer an Exceptional Event

Climate variability is increasing the likelihood of intense rainfall events across the Himalayan region. The September 2024 storm delivered extraordinary rainfall over a short period, overwhelming rivers, drainage systems, and natural waterways. What was once considered a rare event may become increasingly common in the future.

This means that historical assumptions about flood frequency and design standards may no longer be sufficient. Infrastructure planning must begin incorporating future climate risks rather than relying solely on past records.

### Lesson 2: Urban Growth Has Increased Flood Exposure

Kathmandu Valley has undergone rapid urban expansion over the last few decades. Open spaces, wetlands, agricultural land, and natural infiltration areas have gradually been replaced by buildings, roads, and paved surfaces.

As impermeable surfaces increase, rainfall that once infiltrated into the ground now becomes surface runoff. At the same time, settlements and infrastructure have expanded into flood-prone areas along rivers and streams. These trends significantly increase both flood magnitude and exposure. Community discussions following the flood repeatedly highlighted concerns about river encroachment, drainage limitations, and unmanaged urban growth.

![Impacted areas and infrastructure during the September 2024 flood event](/images/blog/kathmandu-flood-3.jpg)

### Lesson 3: Rivers Need Space

Many rivers and streams in Kathmandu have been constrained by encroachment, unmanaged development, and changes in river corridors. During extreme events, rivers naturally seek additional space to convey water and sediment.

The September 2024 flood demonstrated that narrowing river corridors does not eliminate flood risk; it simply transfers that risk to surrounding communities. Effective flood management must include river restoration, protection of floodplains, and enforcement of setback regulations.

### Lesson 4: Flooding Is a Watershed Problem, Not Just a City Problem

Flood risk in Kathmandu is influenced not only by conditions within the city but also by activities occurring upstream. Landslides, sediment transport, catchment degradation, and unregulated extraction activities in surrounding watersheds contributed to the severity of flooding. The disaster highlighted the importance of managing entire river basins rather than focusing exclusively on urban drainage improvements.

A watershed perspective is essential for reducing future flood risk.

### Lesson 5: Early Warning Alone Is Not Enough

Weather agencies issued warnings before the disaster, yet the impacts remained severe. Research and post-disaster assessments indicate that preparedness, response coordination, evacuation planning, and risk communication remain major challenges.

An effective flood early warning system requires more than forecasting. It must ensure that warnings reach vulnerable populations, that communities understand the risks, and that institutions are prepared to act.

### Lesson 6: Nature-Based Solutions Deserve Greater Attention

Traditional flood management often focuses on drainage channels, embankments, and engineered structures. While these measures remain important, they should be complemented by nature-based solutions.

Wetland conservation, urban green spaces, rainwater infiltration systems, river corridor restoration, and watershed rehabilitation can help reduce runoff, improve water retention, and enhance resilience. The September 2024 flood serves as a reminder that working with natural systems can be just as important as building new infrastructure.

![Floodwater and river response in the Kathmandu Valley, September 2024](/images/blog/kathmandu-flood-4.jpg)

## Looking Forward

The September 2024 Kathmandu flood was not merely an extreme weather event. It was a stress test for the valley's urban systems, institutions, and development choices.

The question is not whether another major flood will occur. It is whether Kathmandu will be better prepared when it does.

Reducing future flood risk requires integrated action across urban planning, watershed management, infrastructure development, disaster preparedness, and climate adaptation. The lessons are clear. The challenge now is turning those lessons into action before the next disaster arrives.
